The timeline, laid out

I keep having to re-explain the order of things, so here it is, once, in order. Click a year to open it. Every entry has its own link if you want to send someone straight to one moment.

By Marlowe Hatcher

This is the skeleton I use when I'm writing everything else on this site. Nine points, 1933 to now. Sourced from the same primer I've linked from half my posts — I'm not introducing anything new here, just laying it out so the shape of it is visible at a glance.

Note: entries below describe real violence, briefly. Nothing graphic, but skip the "Serious Murders" and "Coastal Kills" entry if you need to.
1933 Born in Pee Dee country Origin

Donald Henry Gaskins Jr. was born in South Carolina and spent most of his life in and around the Pee Dee region — Florence, Mullins, Prospect, the rural stretch between the piedmont and the coast. This is the geography every later entry happens inside.

1930s–40s A violent childhood Origin

By his own account — which the record treats with real caution, since so much of it comes from his own self-mythologizing later on — his mother was violent, the foster homes he was placed in were violent, and he was sexually abused and raped multiple times. It's the familiar, ugly feedback loop between victimization and offending, and it's worth naming without letting it become an excuse.

1967 Walter Neely, and the first confirmed killing Murder

Gaskins was thirty-six. Walter Neely picked him up hitchhiking, and became his first confirmed killing. It's worth sitting with the direction of that ride — Gaskins was the one who needed the favor. That inversion is most of the method, right there in the first case.

1967–1975 "Serious Murders" and "Coastal Kills" Murder

Over these years, Gaskins killed again and again, and eventually sorted his own killings into two private categories: "Serious Murders" — killing for a reason — and "Coastal Kills" — killing for satisfaction. The actual number is disputed. He confessed to 110. Prosecutors convicted him of 11. He may have killed more than either number, and may have exaggerated some of it too; the confessions themselves were contradictory.

He wasn't a celebrity killer while any of this was happening. He was a chauffeur, a boyfriend, a friend's ex-roommate — someone you might hire for a ride, or meet through a friend of a friend. That's most of why it took as long as it did.

1975 Arrested — for something else entirely Legal

Gaskins was arrested in 1975, not for murder but for hitchhiking fraud. The investigation that followed is what actually led to the murder charges — which is its own small, dark irony: the case that finally caught him wasn't the case that mattered.

1976 Sentenced to death Legal

Convicted of eleven murders — a fraction of what he confessed to — and sentenced to death. His confessions in this period were graphic and inconsistent; some forensic psychologists read him as performing his own criminality for an audience, others as simply telling the truth. Both readings are still live.

1976–1991 Fifteen years on death row Death row

Gaskins spent fifteen years on death row before his execution — and during that time killed at least one other inmate. Whatever else the case is, it isn't a story where incarceration alone resolved anything.

1991 Executed at Broad River Death row

Gaskins was executed by electric chair at Broad River Correctional Institution in Columbia, South Carolina. His final statement showed no remorse and no apology — he spent it criticizing the prison system and airing his own grievance. He left the story exactly as unresolved as it had been the whole time.

2026 A camera, finally Film

Fifty years after the arrest, Blacktop is releasing Gaskins. I wrote about the poster and about why it took this long already. This entry is the one I'll keep updating — trailer, release date, whatever comes next — so if you only bookmark one link on this page, make it this one.

If you're sharing one of these — a screenshot, a link, whatever — the entry that matters most to me is the "Serious Murders" one. Not because it's the most dramatic, but because the count is still uncertain and the uncertainty is itself the point: a lot of the people he killed were people the system wasn't watching closely to begin with.
