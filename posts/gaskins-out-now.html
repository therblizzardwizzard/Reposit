It's out

Ninety-four minutes, digital and on demand, as of this morning. Where to watch it, who actually made it, and one correction I owe you on numbers I've been repeating since April.

By Marlowe Hatcher

Gaskins: The Hitchhiker Killer released today, digital and on demand in the U.S., through Quiver Distribution. Ninety-four minutes, not rated. I haven't watched it yet — tonight, after I finish this post — so this isn't a review. It's the logistics, the people who made it, and a correction.

Where to watch it

IMDb · Rotten Tomatoes · Trailer

It's digital and on demand only for now — no theatrical run I've seen listed. Check whichever storefront you use; Quiver titles usually land across the major ones same-day.

Who made it

I've been writing about this film for months without ever putting the actual credits in one place. Here they are, straight from the press materials:

DirectorChris Helton
WriterJL Goddard
StarringTaylor Stiles (as Donald “Pee Wee” Gaskins), Shena Verrett, Warren Levi Haney, Carlie Maze, James Roseman
ProducersChris Helton, Sydney Helton, Taylor Stiles

One small thing I'd had backwards: I've been calling Blacktop "the distributor" in earlier posts because their name was on the poster. Turns out Blacktop International handled international sales — Quiver Distribution is the one actually putting this in front of U.S. audiences today. Worth knowing if you're trying to find out where else in the world this is landing.

The transformation, on paper

The press kit gives more detail than I expected on Stiles's prep, and it's the kind of detail I actually care about: roughly six months reshaping posture, movement, and voice to play a man nearly a foot shorter, built partly from Gaskins's surviving death-row tapes. He also worked through Final Truth, the 1992 autobiography, treating it — correctly, I'd argue — as the record of a proven liar rather than a reliable source, and trying to separate what's documented from what Gaskins claimed about himself. That's the same instinct I've been trying to apply on this site since April, so I'm glad to see it on the other side of the camera too.

The correction I owe you

I've been citing "confessed to 110, convicted of 11" since my primer in April — it's the number that's floated around true-crime forums and podcasts for years, and I didn't push hard enough on where it actually came from. The film's own press materials are more careful, and more conservative:

  • Confirmed in sworn testimony to at least 13 murders, 1970–1975.
  • Lifetime total, per those same materials, put at at least 15.
  • The case broke in 1975 when police searching for a missing girl unearthed eight bodies in shallow graves near his home in Prospect, S.C.
  • A Florence County jury took 47 minutes to convict him — in one of the murders.
  • The 100+ figure — the one I've been repeating — is the number Gaskins himself claimed before his execution, and it's a number law enforcement and journalists have largely discredited as self-mythology.

I'm not going back to silently edit the old posts — the stat blocks stay as they were, because I said them and I'd rather own the correction than erase it. But if you're quoting a number from this site going forward, use 13 confirmed / at least 15 lifetime, not 110. The gap between what he claimed and what was ever proven is, honestly, more interesting than either number on its own — it's the same gap the film's synopsis leads with.

One line from the press materials I keep turning over: they call him "the man South Carolina came to call 'the Meanest Man in America' — one of the most prolific killers most of the country has never heard of." That's the whole thesis of this blog, in one sentence, and I didn't write it.

Watching tonight. If the film holds up to what the case actually deserves, I'll say so — and if it doesn't, I'll say that too. Need something to post before then? The pocket file has the poster in every shape and a few lines to quote; the timeline has the case in order.
